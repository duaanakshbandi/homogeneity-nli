Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly being used to summarize and verify claims in critical sources that inform consequential decisions. Such claims are full of plural definites. If three of five patients improved after taking drug A, the claim the patients improved after drug A is not true, since two did not, yet its negation, the patients didn't improve after drug A, is not true either, since three did. In formal semantics, this truth-value gap is known as the Homogeneity Gap: both the plural definite and its negation are neither true nor false when the property holds for only a fraction of the plural referent. A model that cannot represent this gap turns mixed evidence into a clean verdict, an error that can be costly in high-stakes use. We introduce HomogeneityNLI, a diagnostic dataset of 1,000 minimal-pair items crossing determiner, polarity, and group composition across ten logical conditions. Evaluating seven open-weight models, we uncover a pervasive Bivalence Bias: models assign plural definites their homogeneous truth conditions. In the zero-shot setting, no model recognizes the gap for both polarities in more than 15% of scenarios, and negated definites are judged False in 84% of cases. Explicit linguistic rules, chain-of-thought, and enumeration prompts produce unstable, model-specific effects that often entrench the bias. Our findings indicate that current open-weight LLMs acquire the truth conditions of plural predication but not its trivalent logic, and cannot yet be trusted to summarize or verify claims whose evidence is mixed.

Why This Matters

AI systems are increasingly being given real decision-making roles. Utah, for example, has become the first state to let an AI examine patients and prescribe medication without a doctor signing off on each prescription [1].

Many of today's AI systems are built on large language models, which learn from text, and language is complex. For example, a claim can be neither true nor false when the evidence is mixed, a phenomenon known in linguistics as homogeneity. Adults draw this distinction readily [2].

Our study shows that LLMs have not acquired this understanding. None of the models we tested shows the adult pattern as its dominant one, and some interpret these statements the way a subgroup of 4–5-year-olds does [3].

So as we hand AI more decisions and less human oversight, we should ask whether it fully understands the language it acts on (Nakshbandi et al., 2026). If this topic interests you, explore the project below and read our paper on arXiv.

The Homogeneity Gap

With a distributive predicate, a plural definite is true when every member has the property, false when none has it, and neither when the group is mixed (Löbner 1987; Schwarzschild 1994; Križ 2015).

⟦the X P⟧ = {
1if ∀x ∈ X : P(x) 0if ∀x ∈ X : ¬P(x) #otherwise

Symmetrically for the X not-P. In a mixed group, complementarity fails: ⟦the X not-P⟧ ≠ 1 − ⟦the X P⟧. Quantifiers keep it: ⟦not all the X P⟧ = 1 − ⟦all the X P⟧.

The gap has been derived as a presupposition, as supervaluation over an existential and a universal reading, and as recursive strengthening by implicature. Tieu, Križ & Chemla (2015) classify answers to the positive and negated definite in a mixed situation into three profiles:

HomogeneousRejects both sentences. Most adults.
Existentialthe = some: accepts the positive, rejects the negated. A subgroup of 4–5-year-olds.
Universalthe = all, negation over it: rejects the positive, accepts the negated. Some adults.

A binary judgment task cannot tell not true from false. With three response options, “reject both” splits into a genuine gap (Neither, Neither), a mixed pattern and a bivalent collapse (False, False).

Analysis

Truth conditions are right; the truth value is wrong

In chain-of-thought rationales that end in False, models restate the positive definite as a universal claim 92% of the time (“The hypothesis claims that all cows are in the barn. Since one cow is not in the barn, the hypothesis is false.”). For the negated definite, 48% restate it as none.

The rationales name exactly the conditions under which the theories predict truth, and then treat their failure as falsity: models map not true onto false.

Tolerance near the top, and only on one side

When all but one member has the property, models accept the positive definite in 25% of cases, compared with 9% for balanced groups and 6% when only one member has it, mirroring non-maximality in human language.

The tolerance is one-sided: when only one member has the property, the negated definite is accepted in just 10% of cases, barely more than for balanced groups (9%). The gap is recognized most often for maximally mixed groups (56% Neither on A, vs. 30–34% otherwise).

Predicates shift which classical value models pick

Action predicates are accepted in 23% of mixed scenarios (The patients improved after taking drug A), properties in only 2% (The hearts were red), and locations and states in 14% each.

Predicate class does not affect the use of Neither: semantics changes which classical value models choose, not whether they recognize the gap.
